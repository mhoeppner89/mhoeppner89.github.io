# Political Targets and Choice Under Uncertainty

*Evidence from German and U.S. Political Actions*

**Martin Höppner**[^1]  
November 15, 2026

## Abstract

Politicians pursue targets: elections to win, votes to enter parliament, coalitions to hold. I examine how the rewards attached to these targets affect their risk taking. Below a target, politicians may accept new ways to lose for a chance of a large gain; above it, they may give up beneficial opportunities to protect their position. In a public-investment model I show that the same political reward can lead a politician to take too much risk or invest too little, even when the politician otherwise shares citizens’ interests. In the model, citizens judge the politician after observing the outcome, and the investment cannot be undone while extensions also cover legislative, coalition, party and office targets. I examine 1,003 documented political actions in Germany and the United States between 2005 and 2026 which provide observational data that points in the predicted direction, although some estimates are weak. I conclude by discussing possible safeguards against conflicts between political success and public welfare.

**Keywords:** political incentives; targets; choice under uncertainty; elections; democratic design

**JEL codes:** D72; D81; P16

# Introduction

Why does half a percent of votes matter? In winner-take-all electoral systems, 49.6 percent of the vote is very different from 50.1 percent. Similar thresholds also matter in getting into parliament, keeping a coalition together or passing a bill. Political competition has been primarily seen as an effective way to align political actors with the citizens they serve, but actual institutional arrangements also affect political actors' preference for actions in situations with uncertain outcomes.

In the final moments of a high-pressure game, a team down by a single goal faces a tough call. They might send the keeper forward for a corner, knowing full well they are risking another goal against. On the other side, a leading team will usually pull their attackers in to hold onto the lead. The aim to win has not shifted, but the team's position relative to the winning line changes its behavior. But if the coach receives most of the reward from winning while the club and its supporters bear much of the loss, we have a problem of misaligned incentives.

The same logic applies to political targets. Below one, a politician might be tempted to undertake actions that have a chance to achieve a large gain while introducing new sources of loss. Above the target, the politician might decide to take actions to protect the current situation or even withdraw from the game if she sees no chance of reaching the target anymore. The pattern is consistent with March (1988), who argue that the reward for success changes the payoff to uncertainty while the actor's underlying preferences remain stable. The electoral accountability that gives politicians these incentives can be an important disciplinary force (Maskin and Tirole 2004), but for a given choice, it creates a distortionary incentive. The reason is that the politician and the people might not be equally aligned in the payoffs from success and failure (including inaction when a better action exists). The logic is summarized in Figure 1.

To illustrate these ideas, let's look at two recent German examples. The liberal party FDP fell short to clear a 5% threshold to get into parliament in the 2025 election. Shortly before the election, when the polls were predicting the shortfall, they added a number of novel ideas in order to appeal to new voters, including allowing crypto ETFs and allowing the ECB and the German Bundesbank to hold bitcoin in their reserves (Free Democratic Party 2025). On the other side, Friedrich Merz, leader of the conservative CDU, had won only a narrow majority with his coalition, 328 out of 630 seats in the Bundestag (German Bundestag 2025). Merz promised an "autumn of reforms", but emphasized that a key challenge was to ensure that reforms had broad enough support (German Federal Government 2025).[^2] Their coalition agreement failed to take a number of obvious opportunities for meaningful reforms, including leaving a number of costly pension promises untouched, kicking the ball down the road on important questions about the central pension by sending them to a commission, and failing to make reforms to control costs in the health and care systems (Fuest 2025).

Other actions take risk seeking even further by attacking the rule that determines political survival. For example, the U.S. House January 6 Committee documented Donald Trump's effort to stop Congress from certifying the electoral result after his defeat (Select Committee to Investigate the January 6th Attack on the United States Capitol 2022) - an action that I would call going all-in to stay in power. Similarly, South Korea's Constitutional Court found that President Yoon Suk-yeol, whose support had declined previously and who faced possible impeachment (Associated Press 2024), used martial law without the required substantive or procedural basis during his conflict with the National Assembly; and ultimately was removed him from office (Constitutional Court of Korea 2025). These cases illustrate the danger to democratic institutions when individual and social motives are not aligned.

Targets affecting choice under uncertainty is a well-studied subject since Prospect Theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979). March (1988) apply this to risk taking around aspirations, and Diecidue and Ven (2008) incorporate the chances of success and failure into expected utility. My point is that in the political setting, a reward for reaching a target gives an actor a reason to value success separately from the other consequences of an action. An underdog can, depending on the voting rule, prefer a wider range of possible vote shares and a favorite a narrower one even if the expected support is the same, as shown in Rose-Ackerman (1991). Kräkel, Nieken, and Przemeck (2014) add campaign investment and laboratory evidence of gambling by disadvantaged challengers and caution by advantaged candidates. Their model also allows risk to affect campaign effort and an opponent's incentives. A closely related trade-off in MPs' leadership choices is studied by Langtry et al. (2026) - they show that even at the expense of leader quality MPs with weaker re-election prospects may support a riskier leader, while MPs with stronger prospects tend to favour a safer leader. They leave the implications for voters' welfare for future research, which I address here by deriving the welfare costs of both responses.

Because whether such choices benefit or harm citizens depends on their consequences. Lizzeri and Persico (2009) show that candidates may take excessive risk when electoral incentives do not reflect the risk aversion of voters. They also show that candidates may take too little risk, i.e. their policy proposals are very similar. If voters benefit from having a wider menu to choose from, this incentive for similarity implies a welfare loss. An unpublished welfare analysis comes from Cugnon de Sévricourt (2025), who shows that re-election incentives can lead a politician to choose excessive policy risk, even when both the politician and voters prefer less risk. His analysis focuses on the cost of taking too much risk. But as previously stated, we also need to focus on the cost of taking too little risk when a worthwhile investment is available. Imagine a situation where a politician decides how much to invest in an infrastructure project. A larger project brings larger gains for voters if it succeeds (and thus increases approval) but larger losses if it fails (decreasing approval). Let's assume for a moment that if there was no reward for winning an election, the politician and citizens would prefer the same level of investment, and suppose that voters judge the politician after the outcome is known, when the investment can no longer be undone. A politician who is behind may now choose a larger project, to gain a chance of winning. A politician who is ahead may choose a smaller project, to protect the lead. So the same reward for success can produce both outcomes and reasons for welfare loss as political standing changes, even when the politician and citizens otherwise would prefer the same option.

Political choices of course also depend on what voters learn from policy. Majumdar and Mukand (2004) show that a politician may continue a failing policy if changing course makes them appear incompetent, and Bils and Izzo (2025) show that policy experiments can change what voters learn and affect the chance of re-election. In the latter model, a more secure incumbent can sometimes experiment more. Much like a poker player who is further ahead can afford to lean into strategies that lead to more risk, but are (on average) better to maximize their winnings. In my model, this translates to the target becoming less important to the politician's decisions if they cannot lead to immediate failure.

This is very similar to Goodhart's law that once a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (Goodhart 1975). The risk behavior I study here is, in a sense, a mechanism for gaming electoral competition and its institutions. The rules reward someone to win a very specific certain vote share, such as the five-percent hurdle to enter parliament in Germany instead of focusing on overall (and long-term) voter approval. So optimizing for that rule can lead individual choices to be misaligned with social welfare.

In the following sections, I first develop a model of how the reward for reaching a target affects incentives for risk behavior (Section 2), I then compare documented actions in Germany and the United States to see whether they vary with position as the model suggests (Section 3). Finally, I discuss how institutions might reduce harmful incentives while preserving the incentive for politicians to care about the voters in the first place (Section 4).

# Political Choice Around Targets

## Individual choice

Everyone has their price. If the payoff of achieving a goal is high enough then an action can be objectively worse in every dimension but its probability of achieving that goal and still be individually rational. Suppose a political actor chooses an action $a$ from a feasible set $\mathcal A$ which produces an uncertain political outcome $X_a$, such as vote share, seats, coalition support, or legislative votes. Let $\theta$ be the target and the actor's perceived chance of success

$$
p(a)=\Pr(X_a\geq \theta).
\tag{1}
$$

Now, let $R(a)$ be the actor's expected payoff from every consequence other than achieving the goal. This could include policy, reputation, resources, political support, or an altruistic motive towards their voters, evaluated using the actor's beliefs. Let $B>0$ be the value of winning (e.g. getting into or remaining in office) or reaching the relevant political goal. The actor's expected payoff is then

$$
U(a)=R(a)+B p(a).
\tag{2}
$$

For two actions, $a$ and $b$, the actor chooses $a$ when

$$
\Delta U=\Delta R+B\Delta p\geq 0,
\tag{3}
$$

where each difference is the value under $a$ minus the one under $b$. If $a$ has worse consequences apart from target success, $\Delta R<0$, but a higher chance of success, $\Delta p>0$, it remains (weakly) preferable as long as

$$
B\geq \frac{-\Delta R}{\Delta p}.
\tag{4}
$$

Holding everything else fixed, a larger reward makes an action with a higher chance of success more attractive relative to one with a lower chance. The success probability of the politician's optimal action is weakly increasing in the reward while expected payoff from the action's other consequences is weakly decreasing (See Appendix A.3 for the proof).

To compare the politician's choice with public welfare, consider two things the public may value: the consequences of an action and whether the politician reaches the target[^3]. As in the public infrastructure example from the introduction, a project may benefit citizens greatly in expectation but cost the politician the deciding vote if it fails badly enough.

A conflict arises when the politician is willing to choose $a$ over $b$, even though public welfare would be higher under $b$. Let us start with a benchmark in which the politician and citizens have the same beliefs and are fully aligned. The politician values all consequences for public welfare, including the future effects of who holds office. An additional private reward for reaching the target can then move the politician only (weakly) away from the public's preferred action.

Without full alignment, a reward can encourage effort to achieve that reward. Suppose a politician can work to improve a public service or alternatively adjust their risk behavior. Either action may increase the chance of success, $p$, relative to the current course. Both can also lower the politician's other payoff, $R$, i.e. effort takes work, while protecting a lead by shunning away from a (for the politician) risky reform can mean giving up benefits the politician values. For each action, the rational politician would compare the gain in expected political reward with its other net costs, as in Equation (3). When comparing the two alternatives, the politician prefers the one with the higher overall payoff. The reward thus creates an incentive to adjust risk behavior as well as to make an effort.

The core logic is that target position changes the value of uncertainty[^4]. For actions that differ in the spread of their possible political outcomes, being behind can make a wider range of outcomes preferable, if it increases the probability of a sufficient gain to cross the target, while being ahead that same volatility can increase the probability of missing that target. Figure 2 shows that improving the probability of target success can justify a lower expected political outcome. In Appendix A.1 I isolate the effect of spread while holding that expectation fixed.

## Welfare analysis

The politician's choice rule above says that he is willing to tolerate some loss if there is a bigger chance of success. To compute the implication of this for citizens, let's go back to the infrastructure investment. If we assume the politician and the citizens have the same beliefs and preferences about the investment, and if there is no separate choice of effort, we can isolate how the reward affects investment.

Suppose a politician chooses an investment $q\in[0,1]$, where one is the largest feasible project. We can denote the net return on each unit invested $Z$ with two possible states: it is $h>0$ with probability $\pi\in(0,1)$ and some loss $\ell \in(0,1)$ otherwise. Increasing investment therefore increases both the gain in the good outcome and the loss in the bad outcome.

To find the investment citizens would prefer, let us assume they value the good outcome by the utility gain $\log(1+hq)$ and the bad outcome by $\log(1-\ell q)$, relative to no investment. Since citizens share these valuations and the same beliefs about project outcomes, their expected welfare is 

$$
w(q)=\pi\log(1+hq)+(1-\pi)\log(1-\ell q).
\tag{5}
$$

 Due to concavity we can call $q_0$ the investment amount that maximizes welfare $w(q)$. To make sure over- and underinvestment are both possible I assume $q_0\in(0,1)$. Thus, investing above $q_0$ raises the expected project return but adds more risk than citizens prefer, while investing below $q_0$ gives up benefits whose value exceeds the benefit of less risk. With this we can judge excessive investment and costly restraint against the same benchmark, and get a welfare loss of $\mathcal L(q)=w(q_0)-w(q)$, which is strictly positive for every $q\ne q_0$. Appendix A.4 derives $q_0$ and proves these results.

In decisions under risk or uncertainty, a politician commits to the project before its outcome is known. Let us additionally assume citizens assess the politician after they observe that outcome, and for simplicity to isolate the investment's consequences that the incumbent and a replacement politician have equal future value for citizens, constant across investments and outcomes.[^5]

To connect this assessment to political success, we can write $k$ for the realized gain in utility needed to reach the political target. A positive $k$ means that the politician starts below the target and needs an improvement, whereas a negative $k$ means that a leader can survive some loss in approval. Better initial standing therefore strictly reduces $k$.

Now suppose reaching the target gives the politician an additional private reward $B>0$, measured in the same utility units as public welfare. With $p(q)$ denoting the probability of political success depending on project outcome, the politician's expected payoff, $U(q)$, follows from Equation (2): 

$$
U(q)=w(q)+B p(q),\qquad
p(q)=\Pr\{\log(1+qZ)\geq k\}.
\tag{6}
$$

 At $B=0$, the politician chooses $q_0$, so any departure becomes worthwhile only if the extra expected political reward compensates for the loss of project welfare.

Suppose first that the politician starts so far below the target that even the good outcome at $q_0$ falls short. Reaching the target in that outcome requires $\log(1+hq)\geq k$, so the smallest successful project is $q_H=(e^k-1)/h$, with $q_0<q_H\leq1$ ensuring that this is a feasible expansion. This then increases the chance of political success from zero to $\pi$; the bad outcome becomes worse, but still ends in political defeat. Having found the smallest expansion that reaches the target in the good outcome, we can now use Equation (3) to check whether its extra expected reward exceeds the welfare lost: 

$$
B\pi>\mathcal L(q_H).
\tag{7}
$$

 An expansion that stops short of $q_H$ lowers welfare without improving the chance of success, whereas expanding beyond $q_H$ lowers welfare without any further political gain. The politician should therefore either keep $q_0$ or increase investment just enough to reach $q_H$.

Now in contrast consider a politician who starts above the target, but would lose the lead in the bad outcome at $q_0$. Retaining success in that outcome requires $\log(1-\ell q)\geq k$, so the largest project that preserves the lead is $q_L=(1-e^k)/\ell$, with $q_L<q_0$. Here the reward favours a smaller project since reducing investment to $q_L$ increases the chance of political success from $\pi$ to one, at the cost of benefits in the good outcome, where political success is already secure. The exact threshold now tells us how much investment must be given up by a rational politician to preserve the lead. As before, the politician strictly prefers this change when the extra expected reward covers the welfare lost: 

$$
B(1-\pi)>\mathcal L(q_L).
\tag{8}
$$

 A reduction that stops above $q_L$ gives up project benefits without saving the lead in the bad outcome, whereas reducing investment below $q_L$ gives up further benefits without adding protection. The politician therefore either keeps $q_0$ or reduces investment just enough to reach $q_L$.

In both cases we can prove that these are the best choices over all possible investments (see Appendix A.4). Figure 3 illustrates politicians' rational investment choice as standing improves from left to right (everything else held fixed). Far below the target, expansion can be too costly or simply unable to reach it, so the politician chooses $q_0$. As the deficit narrows, investing more can become worthwhile. Closer to the threshold, however, $q_0$ already succeeds in the good outcome, so extra investment gives no benefit to the politician. Above the target, the politician can instead protect the lead, but protecting a very small lead requires giving up almost the whole project. If $B(1-\pi)<w(q_0)$, the political benefit is too small to justify that loss, so protection becomes worthwhile only when a larger lead allows the politician to keep more of the project. Far enough ahead, $q_0$ succeeds in both outcomes and the politician returns to the investment citizens prefer. For a higher reward $B$, the regions for welfare reducing behavior expand.

In these comparisons there is also an upper limit on the expected welfare loss the politician will accept. Changing investment can secure political success in at most one additional outcome: the good outcome through expansion, or the bad outcome through underinvestment. The extra expected reward is therefore limited to that outcome's probability times the private reward. Since this gain must cover the welfare lost, the politician's optimal investment $q^*$ satisfies 

$$
0\leq\mathcal L(q^*)\leq
\begin{cases}
B\pi & \text{below the target},\\
B(1-\pi) & \text{at or above the target}.
\end{cases}
\tag{9}
$$

 A smaller $B$ lowers these upper bounds and, as the comparison in Section 2.1 shows, also weakly reduces the welfare loss from the politician's choice, holding everything else fixed. This gives a reason to limit the private reward in cases where effort is not as relevant as adaption to risk behavior[^6]. At some standings, only a slight change in investment is needed to secure political success in one additional outcome, so the welfare cost can be small enough for the extra expected reward to cover it. For simplicity, here I kept politician's assessment of the project's chance of success and its possible gains and losses fixed as political standing changes. The discussion of different beliefs in Appendix A.4 shows that the same investment can still be optimal without the reward under more optimistic or more pessimistic project beliefs, so investment alone cannot distinguish beliefs from incentives.

## Extensions

Whereas the investment example describes a process where the rules of the contest are fixed and hence there is just one possible path to achieving the target (i.e. winning the election), in reality, some of the actors may attempt to change these rules themselves. If the overall goal is to get into or stay in power, winning the established election is one route. But when the standard route to that goal is blocked, overly costly, or uncertain, an actor may take a different (and uncertain) action to get to it by altering the conditions for success - for instance, by gerrymandering or attempting to override the rules governing certification or imposing martial law. The Trump and Yoon cases illustrate extreme attempts to alter or override institutional constraints in pursuit of political control. A less extreme case is reported by Baskaran and Fonseca (2016) where the established parties change the political institutions to their advantage against possible new competitors. After the German state Hesse abolished the five-percent threshold for local elections, council size was shrunk which raised the implicit threshold for winning one seat on the council. A related question is whether an executive breaks the law to pursue political, policy or personal goals. Farber and Gould (2026) examine these incentives and the possible responses of other institutions, when courts and other institutions can block the benefits of lawbreaking or impose costs on the executive. This can reduce the extra expected political reward or increase the action's other costs, as in Equation (3). Of course, breaking a rule differs from changing it, but either can affect the conditions for political success. When used to weaken political competition, such actions damage democratic institutions, contribute to democratic backsliding (Bermeo 2016), and can erode public confidence in the democratic process (Williamson and Justwan 2025).

Political actors often have to balance multiple goals simultaneously. A candidate may need both the support of the general public and the approval of the party; or a chancellor may have to weigh policy goals against the need to keep a coalition intact. Models of electoral choice already show that candidates might value policy outcomes as well as winning (Wittman 1983). Similarly, $R(a)$ in Section 2.1 can incorporate the expected reward from pursuing a second goal, but it does not isolate the effect of a specific goal on risk taking without expansion. Whether it is enough to consider one target depends on how the available actions affect the others since even if there is only one dimension, there can be conflicting incentives. For example, a party may gamble to move from second to first place, while putting second place at risk, so the extra expected reward for first place competes with the expected loss of falling below second. If goals are on different dimensions (e.g. a campaign appeal that wins votes may undermine the politician's standing within the party) and each target produces its own reward, we can assume that a rational politician weighs these gains and losses against each other. However, party approval could also be a condition for reaching office, so votes and office are not always interchangeable goals. In Strøm (1990), votes matter because they lead to office benefits and policy influence. This link, especially direct in competitive two-party contests, helps to explain why an electoral target can be informative, but can be complicated by coalition bargaining, where parties have to choose electoral positions with the later policy negotiations in mind (Austen-Smith and Banks 1988). Thus, we can focus on one target by itself if the other success probabilities do not change across the available actions, or if the candidate has already secured other requirements for office, like the party nomination. So in general, if office requires both electoral victory and coalition agreement, but the chance of agreement after victory is the same under all actions, this simply scales the reward for winning. Yet when an action changes the probability of multiple outcomes, we must take each of them into account.

Finally, the model can also account for the decision to stop trying. Every political pursuit has an outside option, i.e. the value of directing effort elsewhere. An actor continues to pursue a target as long as the best expected payoff from continuing outweighs the value of giving up. A politician may prefer a gamble to a cautious action but still prefer to withdraw, or a target may be out of reach even with effort, gambling, or too costly to pursue - so when another goal offers a higher expected payoff than the best remaining attempt, the politician redirects their time and effort.

## Implications

The model developed above suggests four comparisons of observed actions: risk taking should be more common close below than close above a target when available risky actions increase the chance of reaching it; protection should be more common close above than close below when available actions reduce a threat to success; withdrawal should be more commonly observed far below than close below if a lower chance of success makes continuing less worthwhile than pursuing another goal; and fourth, a larger reward should make politicians more willing to accept sacrifice.

Additionally, we need to account for the time until the election since a deadline could strengthen these incentives. Suppose a politician can make more than one choice before the deadline, so that with enough time left, they could still close a gap or recover from a loss by taking further actions afterwards. As the deadline approaches, fewer such opportunities are left, so the current decision can have a greater effect on the chance of success. Furthermore the politician's position in relation to the target is less ambiguous as the deadline approaches. This can make risk taking below the target or protection above worth a greater sacrifice. If instead the remaining actions cannot reach the target at an acceptable cost, withdrawal should become more attractive, while a larger reward can instead make it worthwhile to keep trying - even when the chance of success is low.

These are conditional predictions and depend on the available actions, their costs, and the reward for success. As we have seen in Figure 3, positions on either side of the target can leave the preferred investment unchanged, so position alone does not necessarily determine the politician's choice.

# Evidence

I examine 1,003 documented political actions across elections in 2005--2026, spanning municipal, state, and national politics in Germany and the United States, using AI assisted internet search and subsequent coding each with human validation (see Appendices B.1 and B.6). For 535 of the actions the coders could determine the position of the actor relative to the target, and of these, 466 can be used in at least one of the three position comparisons; the others were at the target or far above it. Those with an unknown position can still be used in the sacrifice comparison. Only information available before the action is used to determine the actor's target, position, and action, and the cases were included only if a responsible actor took a documented action that was linked to an electoral or office target.

For each action, the coders recorded whether it involved:

-   *Risk taking*: increasing variance if the action creates both a new possible gain and a new possible loss, e.g. when a politician advocates a policy that goes against their core voters' views to attract new ones.

-   *Protection*: decreasing variance if the action gives up a specific opportunity to block a concrete threat to an existing asset, e.g. when a politician drops a reform to avoid losing core voters.

-   *Withdrawal*: the actor stops pursuing the target.

The three position comparisons for both countries are reported in Table 1 with the observed actions fitting the predicted pattern: risk taking is more common close below than close above the target in both countries. The protection and withdrawal differences are clearest in the United States, while in Germany, there are only two of 72 actions close above the target involving protection, none below - which points to the limits of this data set.

I count an action as accepting sacrifice when information available before the action shows policy value, reputation, resources, or average political support put at risk or given up. This is of course only an indirect proxy to test if the expected payoff of the chosen action was lower and due to the observational nature of the data, we cannot hold the rest of the choice parameters fixed. So, we have to rely on average between-subject effects. I code stakes or reward high when the actor faces significant personal consequence, such as losing office or failing to enter parliament. In the data this appears in 70.8 percent of high-stakes German cases and 58.6 percent of lower-stakes cases ($p=0.006$). For the U.S., these rates are 36.0 and 23.3 percent ($p=0.014$). To examine how the size of the reward affects risk behavior and withdrawal rates, I then compare high- and lower-stakes cases within the same position group. Close below the target, risk taking is more common under high stakes in both Germany (71.9 versus 66.7 percent) and the United States (37.5 versus 11.1 percent). Close above the target, protection is also more common under high stakes in the United States (19.7 versus 0.0 percent), while Germany has only two protection cases (as discussed above) with one action in each stakes group. Withdrawal on the other hand should be less common with a higher reward. The data shows this for Germany (18.8 versus 54.5 percent), but not for the United States (71.4 versus 42.9 percent), however, the U.S. lower-stakes groups are small and include only seven cases far below the target (check Appendix B.5 for the exact counts and additional comparisons).

As the deadline approaches, the number of opportunities to close a gap or recover from a loss shrinks, which can make it more attractive to take risk below the target, opt for protection above it, or to withdraw if the remaining actions are unable to reach the target at an acceptable cost. For this check, I use time remaining as a proxy to compare the cases in the same position group that have 30 days or less remaining to those with more time. The results show no common pattern. Risk taking close below the target is less common near the deadline in both countries, while protection close above and withdrawal far below are more common near the deadline in Germany but less common in the United States. The sources do not show whether 30 days or less remained in 97 of 500 German cases and one of 503 U.S. cases. Some decisions, such as forming a government, had no set date when the politician acted. In other cases, the reported dates could put the action either within the last 30 days or earlier. I keep cases with small date differences when it is still clear which of these applies (see Appendix B.5).

Fewer remaining chances can also make it worth accepting greater sacrifice to improve the prospect of success. Here, however, the results depend on how many days count as a short deadline. Comparing cases with 90 days or less remaining against those with more time left, sacrifice is 7.1 percentage points more common in Germany ($p=0.131$) and 13.5 points more common in the United States ($p=0.019$), but when I use 30 days instead, the differences are $-5.1$ points in Germany ($p=0.787$) and 2.3 points in the United States ($p=0.359$). So overall the time effects are inconclusive.

The position comparisons and the higher frequency of sacrifice under high stakes fit the proposed incentives with these differences remaining positive even when each actor receives equal weight or one study period is omitted - to check if a few repeatedly observed politicians or a particular period drive these findings. Collecting at least 500 episodes per country was meant to counter the AI-assisted search's tendency to favor overly prominent or salient cases, but of course both reporting (media bias) and retrieval (AI selection bias) may still favor particular types of cases. For example, a gamble by a trailing politician could be easier to observe than a cautious choice, with the latter one making sacrifice particularly hard to code without documented alternatives. These biases could strengthen or weaken the observed pattern (Meng 2018).

# Institutional Implications

Earlier research and the evidence from Germany and the United States are consistent with electoral and legislative targets shaping how politicians take risks, possibly at expense of other outcomes citizens value (Lizzeri and Persico 2009; Kräkel, Nieken, and Przemeck 2014; Langtry et al. 2026). But Democracy needs electoral and legislative targets to align politicians to voters' preferences in the first place (Maskin and Tirole 2004) - so, the question we need to address is how to limit conflicts between political success and public welfare while preserving those incentives.

**Reward.** A first instinct could be to reduce the reward that a narrow victory gives over defeat, making it less worthwhile for a politician to sacrifice social welfare just to improve the chance of success (Equation (4)). In the investment model, a smaller prize $B$ lowers the upper bounds on the welfare loss from gambling ($B\pi$) and protection ($B(1-\pi)$), so the chosen loss can only fall or stay the same when everything else is held fixed[^7]. But reducing the reward can also weaken useful effort and alignment, since the prospect of re-election rewards work that voters cannot observe directly (Ferejohn 1986), as in classic principal-agent problems. The way a reward rises therefore matters as well as its size. Ideally, a reward that rises at a constant rate with citizens' realized utility - motivating effort without tempting the politician to distort the investment[^8] - would be ideal. In practice, institutions can move closer to a gradual reward by having political influence rise more smoothly with support. For example, there are opposition rights over information, committees, or the agenda which preserve some influence for the losing side (Wegmann 2022), and proportional representation allows a party gaining from 30 to 35 percent of the vote to win extra seats, even if it stays in opposition (Lizzeri and Persico 2001, 2009).

Still, proportional representation does not eliminate these distortions. Thresholds like Germany's entry hurdle and coalition majority keep some votes decisive and proportional seats do not translate into a constant-rate reward. Moreover, while flatter rewards lower the worst-case welfare loss, they come with their own trade-offs (Appendix A.5): they can induce investment changes at standings where a sharp threshold would have left the optimal project alone, dull the incentive to exert effort, and (when power is shared) make it harder for voters to know whom to hold responsible (Powell and Whitten 1993).

**Reversibility.** Citizens' losses also depend on the reversibility of a decision, especially when uncertainty is high. The use of pilot programs, step-by-step rollouts, and expiry dates allows a government to test a policy while retaining a route back to the prior state. By capping the potential losses from a failure, a pilot makes experimentation more attractive for the politician[^9]. Variation in policy produces information and reversibility allows for subsequent decisions to make use of what has been learned (Listokin 2008). A pilot should therefore specify its scale, the evidence to collect, and what happens when the pilot ends - otherwise, it can turn into a permanent policy without public attention, or the program's renewal date can turn into another all-or-nothing political contest.

**Transparency.** In the investment model, distortions arise because voters judge the politician by the final outcome rather than the quality of the initial decision. If voters instead could judge the options using only the information available when the decision was made (so that choosing the best project for citizens never hurt the politician's chances), there would be no reason to deviate from the public interest. Independent assessments could help voters evaluate decisions this way. For example, research service could compare policy proposals against alternatives and the status quo to help voters distinguish decision from an uncertain outcome. The ifo assessment of Merz's coalition agreement warned that its pension promises would require more tax funding, leaving less room for tax relief on work and investment, while cost-saving reform was left to a commission (Fuest 2025). The Dutch CPB, which analyzes election manifestos, and the UK Office for Budget Responsibility, which audits government policy costings, offer additional precedents (CPB Netherlands Bureau for Economic Policy Analysis 2021; Office for Budget Responsibility n.d.), however effort and time required to engage with this information make it individually costly. Public algorithms and AI could fill such a role in the future (Höppner 2026).

**Direct democracy.** If politicians' and voters' preferences are not aligned, citizen initiatives and referendums give voters a direct way to intervene. A referendum can let voters veto a self-serving rule change or policy gamble, or ratify a needed reform. Where possible this should happen before irreversible costs are locked in (Cheneval and el-Wakil 2018). Even the threat of a referendum can discipline politicians across decisions beyond the measures actually put to a vote (Le Bihan 2018), particularly where representatives and the public disagree most (Leemann and Wasserfallen 2016). Regular opportunities to decide policy (ideally supported by the independent assessments above) can also give citizens stronger incentives to learn about the alternatives (Benz and Stutzer 2004; Petitpas and Sciarini 2025), however, we have to acknowledge the trade-off between informedness and the costs to do so.

Of course, direct democracy creates its own target. A binary referendum forces a complex debate into a yes-or-no threshold. Limited stakes and reward for any single referendum, repeated political engagement (so that learning is possible), clearly communicated trade-offs, neutral wording, and multi-option ballots can counteract this problem and introduce a more incremental reward for the politicians as laid out above.

**Rule protection.** Finally, a short love letter to checks and balances. By reducing the extra chance of success, $\Delta p$, or raising the action's costs, such safeguards can make changing the rules no longer worthwhile (Equation (3)). In redistricting, for example, Cox (2006) proposes delaying the use of legislators' maps for several elections to weaken the link to their own careers. Delaying a change, however, can leave a defective rule in place in the meantime. Judicial review, on the other hand, offers a more immediate check, e.g. Germany's 2023 electoral reform abolished the exception that allowed parties winning three local districts to enter parliament below the five-percent threshold. In July 2024, the Federal Constitutional Court upheld the core reform but struck down the threshold without an exception and required the three-district rule to remain in effect until the law was revised (Federal Constitutional Court of Germany 2024). The aim should be to prevent democratic backsliding, while still allowing reforms that have broad support, while the final decision should not be made by the immediate benefactors alone.

# Conclusion

Electoral and legislative targets are often used to align politicians with voters. However, the incentives for reaching these targets can distort decisions under uncertainty: trailing politicians may take excessive risks, while leading politicians may avoid useful reforms. Some actors may even try to change the rules in order to stay in power. In the investment model, losses to social welfare occur even when politicians and voters share the same policy goals, because voters judge the politician after they see the final outcome. The 1,003 documented actions from Germany and the United States are consistent with these patterns: risk taking is more common when politicians are close below a target and high stakes increase sacrifice. However, observational data cannot prove that target incentives caused these choices.

Future research must emphasize the separation of target incentives from a politician's beliefs. The reason a politician chooses a risky project may be the desire to cross a target, but it can also just be that the politician just expects the project to succeed. Experiments could be able to distinguish these reasons by recording beliefs before each choice, and changing the politician's standing and reward. No rule is safe from Goodhart's law in the long run. But if we combine lower-stakes rewards, better informed voters, reversible policies and strengthen checks on rule changes, we can limit the negative effect of institutional thresholds on risk behavior and ultimately social welfare.

# Appendix A. Model Details

## Outcome spread

To see how a wider or narrower range of outcomes changes the chance of reaching the target while the expected outcome stays fixed, let the political outcome under action $a$ be

$$
X_a=\mu_a+\sigma_a\varepsilon,
$$

where $\mu_a$ is the expected outcome, $\sigma_a>0$ is its spread, and $\varepsilon$ has a continuously differentiable cumulative distribution $F$ with density $f$. The shock has a finite mean, with $\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0$, so changing the spread leaves the expected outcome $\mu_a$ unchanged. Define $z_a=(\theta-\mu_a)/\sigma_a$. The actor's chance of crossing the target is

$$
p(a)=1-F(z_a).
$$

Holding the expected outcome and target fixed,

$$
\frac{\partial p}{\partial \sigma_a}
=\frac{f(z_a)z_a}{\sigma_a}.
\tag{12}
$$

Where $f(z_a)>0$, a wider spread raises the chance of success when $\mu_a<\theta$, lowers it when $\mu_a>\theta$, and has no first-order effect when $\mu_a=\theta$. The last case implies a 50-percent chance of success only when $F(0)=1/2$, as in a symmetric distribution centered at zero.

Even if a change in spread raises the chance of success, it is worthwhile only if the extra expected political reward covers any loss in the actor's other payoffs, as Equation (3) requires. The calculation above shows how spread changes the chance of success; the payoff comparison determines whether the politician chooses it. Here, each probability enters expected payoff without adjustment. If actors overweight or underweight particular probabilities, as in cumulative prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1992), the prize term would need a probability-weighting function. The observed behavior comparisons do not reveal how actors weight probabilities, so they cannot distinguish these accounts.

## Withdrawal and rule changes

Let $d$ measure how far the actor is below the target, $\mathcal A_T(d)$ be the actions still available before the deadline, $c(d)$ be the cost of continuing, and $V(d)$ be the value of using effort elsewhere. Define the best return from continued pursuit as

$$
M(d)=\max_{a\in\mathcal A_T(d)}
\left\{R_T(a;d)+B p(a;d)-c(d)\right\},
$$

where $R_T$ excludes both the prize $B$ and the continuation cost $c(d)$. The actor withdraws when $M(d)<V(d)$. For risk taking close below the target to give way to withdrawal farther below it, the return from continuing relative to using effort elsewhere, $M(d)-V(d)$, must eventually fall as the gap grows and cross zero. Distance alone does not guarantee withdrawal: a remote target can still attract effort when the prize is large, a new action becomes available, or the outside option is poor.

An actor who continues pursuing the target may also try to change the rule that defines success. For a local change in an action $a$, allow the expected outcome $\mu(a)$, spread $\sigma(a)$, and target $\theta(a)$ all to vary. Then

$$
\frac{d p}{d a}
=\frac{f(z)}{\sigma}
\left[\mu'(a)-\theta'(a)+z\sigma'(a)\right],
\qquad
z=\frac{\theta(a)-\mu(a)}{\sigma(a)}.
\tag{14}
$$

The three terms describe changing the expected political outcome, changing the rule, and changing the spread of possible outcomes. With a fixed prize, $dU/da=R'(a)+B\,dp/da$. If the action also changes the prize, the additional term is $B'(a)p(a)$. Equation (14) describes small changes: lowering the hurdle can improve the chance of success just as raising expected performance can. For attempts to cancel certification or impose martial law, the politician must compare the success probabilities and other payoffs of the whole actions.

## Reward size

To show why a larger reward can make the politician give up other payoffs for a greater chance of success, let $a_1$ and $a_2$ maximize $R(a)+B_i p(a)$ over the same feasible set at $0\leq B_1<B_2$. Beliefs and payoffs apart from the political reward are fixed. Since each action is best at its respective reward, 

$$
R(a_1)+B_1p(a_1)\geq R(a_2)+B_1p(a_2),\quad
R(a_2)+B_2p(a_2)\geq R(a_1)+B_2p(a_1).
$$

 Adding the inequalities gives $(B_2-B_1)[p(a_2)-p(a_1)]\geq0$, so $p(a_2)\geq p(a_1)$. The first inequality then gives $R(a_2)-R(a_1)\leq-B_1[p(a_2)-p(a_1)]\leq0$. The result holds for any best choices, provided they exist. It includes ties and a zero initial reward. This result does not rank public welfare in the general model because it concerns the politician's other payoffs. In the investment model, however, citizens and the politician share the project welfare function, and retention and replacement have equal continuation value for citizens. With returns, beliefs, standing, and feasible investments fixed, a smaller $B$ therefore weakly reduces the welfare loss. This comparison changes the reward alone; an actual reform may also change which actions are available or how opponents respond.

## Investment choice

The valuations in Section 2.2 can be obtained from logarithmic utility over consumption. Citizen $i$ starts with consumption $y_i>0$ and receives $y_iY(q,Z)$ after the project, where 

$$
Y(q,Z)=1+qZ,\qquad
Z=\begin{cases}
h & \text{with probability }\pi,\\
-\ell & \text{with probability }1-\pi.
\end{cases}
\tag{15}
$$

 The utility change relative to initial consumption is $\log[y_iY(q,Z)]-\log y_i=\log(1+qZ)$. The bound $\ell<1$ keeps consumption positive even if the largest feasible project fails. Citizens face the same proportional gains and losses, so different initial consumption levels do not affect their investment ranking.

The interior assumption is 

$$
0<q_0=\frac{\pi h-(1-\pi)\ell}{h\ell}<1.
\tag{16}
$$

 Differentiation gives 

$$
\begin{aligned}
w'(q)&=\frac{\pi h}{1+hq}-\frac{(1-\pi)\ell}{1-\ell q}
      =\frac{h\ell(q_0-q)}{(1+hq)(1-\ell q)},\\
w''(q)&=-\frac{\pi h^2}{(1+hq)^2}
       -\frac{(1-\pi)\ell^2}{(1-\ell q)^2}<0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $w$ increases up to $q_0$ and decreases after $q_0$. Also, $w(0)=0$ and $w(q_0)>0$. The loss is 

$$
\mathcal L(q)=w(q_0)-w(q)
=\pi\log\frac{1+hq_0}{1+hq}
 +(1-\pi)\log\frac{1-\ell q_0}{1-\ell q}.
\tag{17}
$$

 This loss is positive for every $q\ne q_0$. A proportional consumption increase of $e^{\mathcal L(q)}-1$ in both outcomes compensates a citizen for this loss.

An approval rule can give the performance requirement $k$. Citizens have different approval standards with a continuous, strictly increasing cumulative distribution $H$. Each citizen approves when initial standing plus the realized utility gain meets that citizen's standard. For initial standing $b$ and approval target $\theta\in(0,1)$, 

$$
X(q,Z)=H\!\left(b+\log(1+qZ)\right),\qquad
k=H^{-1}(\theta)-b.
\tag{18}
$$

Then $X(q,Z)\geq\theta$ exactly when $\log(1+qZ)\geq k$. Initial approval is $H(b)=X(0,Z)$. Expected approval after investment can differ from initial approval. The rule takes citizens' standards for judging realized outcomes as given; it does not model how those standards emerge from voters' strategic choices.

For $k>0$, the bad outcome never reaches the target. The good outcome reaches it exactly when $q\geq q_H$. Thus 

$$
p(q)=\pi\,\mathbf 1\{q\geq q_H\}.
$$

If $q_0<q_H\leq1$, the best choice below $q_H$ is $q_0$. The best choice at or above $q_H$ is $q_H$. The payoff difference is $B\pi-\mathcal L(q_H)$. This proves Equation (7).

For $k<0$, the good outcome always reaches the target. The bad outcome reaches it exactly when $q\leq q_L$. Thus 

$$
p(q)=\pi+(1-\pi)\,\mathbf 1\{q\leq q_L\}.
$$

If $q_L<q_0$, the best choice at or below $q_L$ is $q_L$. The best choice above $q_L$ is $q_0$. The payoff difference is $B(1-\pi)-\mathcal L(q_L)$. This proves Equation (8).

At equality, both candidate investments maximize the politician's payoff, whereas strict inequalities give a unique choice. The two-choice argument uses the increase in welfare before $q_0$ and its decrease after $q_0$, together with the two project outcomes and the sharp success rule. Log utility provides the formulas, but the same argument applies more generally when welfare has these properties.

Two performance requirements summarize the outcomes at the public optimum: 

$$
k_-=\log(1-\ell q_0)<0,\qquad
k_+=\log(1+hq_0)>0.
$$

 They also equal $\log[(1-\pi)(h+\ell)/h]$ and $\log[\pi(h+\ell)/\ell]$, respectively.

| Position | Political outcomes at $q_0$ | Optimal choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $k\leq k_-$ | Both outcomes reach the target. | $q_0$. |
| $k_-<k<0$ | Only the good outcome reaches it. | $q_L$ if $\mathcal L(q_L)<B(1-\pi)$. Otherwise $q_0$. |
| $0<k\leq k_+$ | The good outcome already reaches it. | $q_0$. |
| $k>k_+$ | Neither outcome reaches it. | $q_H$ if $q_H\leq1$ and $\mathcal L(q_H)<B\pi$. Otherwise $q_0$. |

At equality in either loss comparison, both listed choices are optimal.

At $k=0$, the politician starts exactly at the target, and meeting it exactly counts as success. Zero investment therefore reaches the target with certainty. Every positive investment risks a loss in the bad outcome and succeeds with probability $\pi$, so the choice is between zero and $q_0$. Zero investment is strictly preferred when $B(1-\pi)>w(q_0)$; equality gives two optimal choices.

As a lead narrows toward zero, the largest lead-preserving project $q_L$ shrinks toward zero and its welfare cost rises toward $w(q_0)$. If $B(1-\pi)<w(q_0)$, a very small lead remains unprotected. The protection boundary solves $\mathcal L(q_L)=B(1-\pi)$.

Below the target, $\mathcal L(q_H)$ increases with the required gain once $k>k_+$. If $B\pi<\mathcal L(1)$, a unique cost boundary occurs before the largest feasible project. At that boundary, $\mathcal L(q_H)=B\pi$. With a larger deficit, the politician chooses $q_0$.

If $B\pi>\mathcal L(1)$, gambling remains worthwhile up to $q_H=1$. A larger deficit makes success infeasible. Equality gives a tie at the capacity limit. Thus cost or capacity can stop gambling.

Optimality against the available choice $q_0$ gives 

$$
0\leq\mathcal L(q^*)\leq
B\,[p(q^*)-p(q_0)].
$$

 A strictly preferred departure gives a strict upper inequality. The probability gain is $\pi$ for gambling and $1-\pi$ for protection. This proves Equation (9). Protection also satisfies $\mathcal L(q^*)\leq w(q_0)$.

For two rewards $0\leq B_1<B_2$, the standard revealed-preference comparison gives 

$$
p(q_2)\geq p(q_1),\qquad w(q_2)\leq w(q_1),
$$

 where $q_j$ is any optimal choice at $B_j$. Thus a smaller reward weakly reduces the chosen welfare loss. This comparison holds project returns, beliefs, standing, and feasible choices fixed.

Reducing the reward cannot eliminate both distortions across all standings: for every $B>0$, some standings require so little change in investment that its welfare cost is worth paying. To show this, choose a small $\epsilon>0$ with $q_0\pm\epsilon\in(0,1)$. The requirements 

$$
k_H=\log(1+h(q_0+\epsilon)),\qquad
k_L=\log(1-\ell(q_0-\epsilon))
$$

 give cutoff investments $q_0+\epsilon$ and $q_0-\epsilon$. Their losses approach zero as $\epsilon$ approaches zero. Each fixed positive political gain therefore exceeds the corresponding loss for sufficiently small $\epsilon$. The departure can therefore have an arbitrarily small welfare cost.

The main model gives the incumbent and a replacement equal continuation value. Now suppose that retention gives citizens an additional value $G\geq0$. This value is constant across investments and project outcomes. Public and private expected payoffs become 

$$
W_G(q)=w(q)+Gp(q),\qquad
U_G(q)=w(q)+(G+B)p(q).
$$

 The term $Gp(q)$ varies with investment because investment changes the chance that citizens retain the incumbent they prefer. It therefore counts when citizens compare projects, even though the value of retaining that incumbent is fixed.

In the expansion and protection cases derived above, the same two candidate investments determine both choices. The extra success probability $\Delta p$ is $\pi$ for expansion and $1-\pi$ for protection. The project loss must now be weighed against the public benefit of a higher chance of retaining the incumbent. A conflict arises when that benefit is too small to cover the loss for citizens, but the additional private prize makes the departure worthwhile for the politician. The politician strictly prefers a departure that lowers public welfare exactly when 

$$
G\Delta p<\mathcal L(q)<(G+B)\Delta p.
\tag{19}
$$

Citizens then prefer $q_0$, and the politician prefers the cutoff investment. The total public welfare loss is $\mathcal L(q)-G\Delta p$. If $G\Delta p>\mathcal L(q)$, citizens also prefer the cutoff investment. Equality cases give ties.

[]{#app:equivalent-beliefs label="app:equivalent-beliefs"}

Under log utility, investment changes can resemble changes in beliefs. Define 

$$
\widetilde\pi(q)=\frac{\ell(1+hq)}{h+\ell}.
$$

A decision-maker without the political reward would choose $q$ under the success belief $\widetilde\pi(q)$. At $q_0$, this belief equals $\pi$. A larger investment corresponds to a higher success belief. A smaller investment corresponds to a lower success belief. At $q=0$ or $q=1$, a range of beliefs can support the same investment.

The loss has the exact form 

$$
\mathcal L(q)=
\pi\log\frac{\pi}{\widetilde\pi(q)}
+(1-\pi)\log\frac{1-\pi}{1-\widetilde\pi(q)}.
\tag{20}
$$

 This is the Kullback--Leibler divergence from the true success distribution to the equivalent-belief distribution. The identity follows from 

$$
1+hq=\frac{h+\ell}{\ell}\widetilde\pi(q),\qquad
1-\ell q=\frac{h+\ell}{h}[1-\widetilde\pi(q)],
$$

 and $\widetilde\pi(q_0)=\pi$.

The standard Kelly investment problem has the same expected-log objective (Kelly 1956). Barron and Cover (1988) relate the cost of incorrect probabilities to relative entropy. The fraction lost in the bad outcome is $f=\ell q$, so the conventional Kelly fraction is $f_0=\ell q_0$. Although Kelly's setting concerns repeated investment, the expected-log objective here evaluates a single public project; repetition is not required for the identity.

Actual beliefs stay fixed in this model, but the same investment can be supported by a different belief without the reward. This limits what can be inferred from investment alone. With $G>0$, Equation (20) measures the project welfare gap; total public welfare must also include the effect of retention.

Gambling secures political success in the good outcome, with probability $\pi$. Protection secures it in the bad outcome, with probability $1-\pi$. These are the political benefits in the two conditions. Changing $\pi$ also changes $q_0$, the welfare costs, and the relevant standing regions. A more likely project success therefore changes both the gain from crossing the target and the investment citizens prefer; it need not produce a larger distortion.

If higher $w(q)$ never gives a lower $p(q)$, then $q_0$ maximizes both project welfare and the chance of political success. It therefore also maximizes the politician's payoff. Setting $B=0$ or making $p(q)$ constant also restores $q_0$.

A smooth reward can still change investment. For example, a reward proportional to realized consumption gives expected payoff 

$$
w(q)+B\,\mathbb E[1+qZ].
$$

 Its derivative at $q_0$ is $B[\pi h-(1-\pi)\ell]>0$. Thus a reward discontinuity is sufficient for the cutoff mechanism, but is unnecessary for a welfare conflict.

The timing assumption also affects the public choice. Suppose citizens can learn the eventual outcome and reject the project before any cost occurs. With costless rejection, expected welfare becomes 

$$
\mathbb E[\max\{\log(1+qZ),0\}]=\pi\log(1+hq).
$$

 This increases with investment, so a policy choice before commitment can have a different welfare ranking. The next subsection examines how reward shape and partial reversibility change these comparisons.

## Model extensions

The institutional proposals in Section 4 raise three questions: how the reward rises with outcomes, whether it encourages effort, and what changes when project losses can be avoided. The following extensions examine these questions within the investment model. Write $u(q,Z)=\log(1+qZ)$ for citizens' realized utility gain. A nondecreasing reward schedule $S$ gives the share of the private prize $B$ obtained at each realized gain. On the feasible range of outcomes, $0\leq S\leq1$. Let $\alpha\in(0,1]$ be the weight the politician places on project welfare relative to the private reward. The politician chooses $q\in[0,1]$ to maximize 

$$
U(q)=\alpha w(q)+B\,\mathbb E[S(u(q,Z))].
\tag{21}
$$

 The main model has $\alpha=1$ and $S(u)=\mathbf 1\{u\geq k\}$. A smaller $\alpha$ leaves the preferred investment without a reward unchanged; it increases the importance of the private prize rather than introducing disagreement over the ranking of projects.

With the threshold reward, the candidate investments $q_H$ and $q_L$ are unchanged. The politician strictly prefers the relevant departure from $q_0$ when $B\pi>\alpha\mathcal L(q_H)$ or $B(1-\pi)>\alpha\mathcal L(q_L)$. Optimality against $q_0$ gives 

$$
0\leq\mathcal L(q^*)\leq\frac{B}{\alpha}[p(q^*)-p(q_0)].
$$

 Thus the bounds in Equation (9) become $(B/\alpha)\pi$ and $(B/\alpha)(1-\pi)$. What matters is the prize relative to the politician's own stake in the project.

Suppose $S$ is differentiable. At the interior public optimum, 

$$
\frac{\pi h}{1+hq_0}=\frac{(1-\pi)\ell}{1-\ell q_0}=\frac{h\ell}{h+\ell}.
$$

 Differentiating Equation (21) therefore gives 

$$
U'(q_0)=B\,\frac{h\ell}{h+\ell}\,[S'(k_+)-S'(k_-)],
\tag{22}
$$

 where $k_+=\log(1+hq_0)$ and $k_-=\log(1-\ell q_0)$ are the two outcomes at $q_0$. A small expansion is attractive when the reward is steeper at the good outcome than at the bad one, and a small reduction is attractive in the opposite case. When nonzero, the sign of $U'(q_0)$ tells us which direction of a small change raises payoff at $q_0$. With a general reward schedule and a large prize, a larger change may still offer a greater payoff on either side. Even a zero derivative leaves that possibility open. To determine the best investment across all feasible choices, we therefore need more information about the shape of the reward.

Some schedules give global results. If $S(u)=s_0+\beta u$ with $\beta\geq0$ on the feasible outcome range, then $U(q)=(\alpha+B\beta)w(q)+Bs_0$, so $q_0$ remains optimal for every $B$. The intercept and slope must keep $S$ between zero and one on that range. If instead $S$ is convex, every optimal investment is at least $q_0$; if $S$ is concave, every optimal investment is at most $q_0$. To see this, write $g(q)=\mathbb E[S(u(q,Z))]$. In the convex case, monotonicity and $\log(1+hq)\geq\log(1-\ell q)$ imply 

$$
g'(q)=\frac{\pi hS'(\log(1+hq))}{1+hq}-\frac{(1-\pi)\ell S'(\log(1-\ell q))}{1-\ell q}
\geq S'(\log(1-\ell q))w'(q)\geq0
$$

 for $q<q_0$. Hence $U'(q)>0$ below $q_0$. For concave $S$ and $q>q_0$, both inequalities reverse, so $U'(q)<0$. A reward proportional to realized consumption, as considered in Appendix A.4, is an example of the convex case after rescaling the schedule and prize.

Now keep a twice continuously differentiable schedule $S$ fixed while $B/\alpha$ tends to zero. Since $w$ is strictly concave and its maximum is interior, a sufficiently small perturbation gives a unique interior optimum close to $q_0$. Using 

$$
w''(q_0)=-\frac{h^2\ell^2}{\pi(1-\pi)(h+\ell)^2},
$$

 the first-order condition and a Taylor expansion give 

$$
\begin{aligned}
q^*-q_0&=\frac{B}{\alpha}\,\frac{\pi(1-\pi)(h+\ell)}{h\ell}[S'(k_+)-S'(k_-)]+o(B/\alpha),\nonumber\\
\mathcal L(q^*)&=\frac12\left(\frac{B}{\alpha}\right)^2\operatorname{Var}[S'(u(q_0,Z))]+o((B/\alpha)^2),
\tag{23}
\end{aligned}
$$

 where the variance equals $\pi(1-\pi)[S'(k_+)-S'(k_-)]^2$. For a fixed smooth schedule, the leading welfare loss is therefore quadratic in the reward relative to the welfare weight, whereas the threshold model permits worst-case losses across standings of order $B/\alpha$. At a fixed standing, the threshold model can leave $q_0$ optimal for sufficiently small prizes. These comparisons do not establish that smoothing reduces the loss at every standing, or cover a schedule that becomes steeper as the prize shrinks.

For illustration, take the project of Figure 3, $\alpha=1$, and the logistic schedule $S(u)=1/(1+e^{-(u-k)/\tau})$, with scale $\tau>0$. On a grid of requirements $k\in[-1.5,1.5]$, the largest loss with $B=0.20$ is approximately 0.100 under the threshold reward, compared with 0.084, 0.057, 0.020, and 0.002 for $\tau=0.02,0.05,0.1,0.3$. At some standings, however, the preferred investment already gives the best chance of success under the threshold, so changing investment earns no extra prize. For example, at $k=0.3$, the threshold leaves $q_0$ optimal. The gradual reward still makes a change worthwhile: $\tau=0.1$ gives a loss of about 0.0046. On the same grid, the average loss for $\tau=0.02$ is slightly higher than under the threshold. All schedules have range zero to one, but flattening them can reduce the marginal reward at the outcomes actually faced. Effort is held fixed in this calculation. A flatter reward can reduce losses while giving less reason to work, so these numbers do not compare rewards chosen to induce the same effort. The replication script `theory_welfare/reward_schedule_checks.py` reproduces the illustration and checks the local formulas and their limits.

Let effort $e\in[0,\bar e]$, with finite $\bar e>0$, raise citizens' utility gain by the same amount in both outcomes. Its private cost $C$ is twice continuously differentiable, increasing for $e>0$, and strictly convex, with $C'(0)=0$ and $C'(\bar e)>1$. Require $0\leq S\leq1$ on the full feasible range $[\log(1-\ell),\bar e+\log(1+h)]$. The politician maximizes 

$$
U(q,e)=\alpha[e+w(q)]-C(e)+B\,\mathbb E[S(e+u(q,Z))].
$$

 For a continuously differentiable schedule, continuity and compactness ensure that a best choice exists, and an interior effort choice satisfies 

$$
C'(e)=\alpha+B\,\mathbb E[S'(e+u(q,Z))].
\tag{24}
$$

 The direct reward for extra effort depends on the average marginal reward because effort improves both outcomes. At $q_0$ and a given effort, the investment derivative instead depends on the difference $S'(e+k_+)-S'(e+k_-)$. The local loss expansion applies with effort fixed. Here, effort adds equally to both outcomes. Effort that instead changes the chance of project success or the spread of returns can also change the investment trade-off, so this calculation does not establish the same separation of incentives for those kinds of effort.

Under an affine schedule, $q_0$ is optimal for every effort and an interior effort choice has $C'(e)=\alpha+B\beta$. If the private effort cost also counts in social welfare, total surplus $e+w(q)-C(e)$ is maximized at $q_0$ and $C'(e)=1$. For $\alpha<1$, a feasible calibration $B\beta=1-\alpha$ restores that effort level without distorting investment. Feasibility requires a slope and intercept that keep the reward share in $[0,1]$ throughout the attainable outcome range. Other slopes can leave effort below or above its efficient level: the reward must make the additional effort worthwhile, not merely rise smoothly. Under the threshold reward, extra effort earns the prize only when it moves an outcome across the requirement. The politician can then compare working harder with changing the scale of investment as ways to reach the same target.

By Equation (18), the approval share is $X=H(b+u)$. Let $\rho$ map that share into the share of the private prize. Then $S(u)=\rho(H(b+u))$ and, where both functions are differentiable, 

$$
S'(u)=\rho'(X)H'(b+u).
$$

 Proportional influence makes $\rho$ linear: each additional vote yields the same extra influence. Yet the number of citizens close to approving, measured by the density $H'$, can differ between the good and bad outcomes. The same improvement in citizens' utility can therefore win different numbers of votes, so a reward proportional to votes need not rise at a constant rate with utility. An entry hurdle or a majority requirement for office can also create a jump in $\rho$.

In the costless-rejection variant at the end of Appendix A.4, citizens reject a failing project before any loss occurs. Welfare becomes $\pi\log(1+hq)$, which increases with investment. For $k>0$, political success occurs only when the good outcome reaches the target; for $k\leq0$, success is certain. In either case its probability is nondecreasing in $q$, so citizens and the politician both choose $q=1$ for every $B$ and $\alpha>0$. Here, failure costs citizens nothing, so their preferred project is also the largest one. An ordinary pilot uses resources while testing a policy and does not offer this complete escape from failure costs.

For partial reversibility, suppose the loss parameter falls from $\ell$ to $\lambda\ell$, with $\lambda\in(0,1]$. The public optimum is 

$$
q_0(\lambda)=\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi h-(1-\pi)\lambda\ell}{h\lambda\ell}\right\}.
$$

 While this optimum is interior, its bad outcome is $\log[(1-\pi)(h+\lambda\ell)/h]$. Reducing $\lambda$ increases the preferred investment and makes this outcome worse: citizens choose more exposure when failure is cheaper. Once the optimum reaches $q=1$, further reductions in $\lambda$ improve its bad outcome. Cheaper failure changes the investment citizens want as well as the losses from any given project. To judge the politician's response, it must therefore be compared with this new preferred investment; a reduction in losses for an unchanged project is not enough.

# Appendix B. Empirical Methods

## Data collection

The search used two separate research threads, one for Germany and one for the United States, each instructed to collect at least 500 verified episodes of political action in an electoral context between 2005 and 2026. Neither supervisor was given the paper's theory, so the search instructions did not ask for examples that supported its predictions. Each supervisor used GPT-5.6 Sol with extra high reasoning effort and delegated the search to GPT-5.6 Luna agents with maximum reasoning effort. Additional GPT-5.6 Luna agents, with the same reasoning setting, checked the candidate episodes against the inclusion requirements. The supervisors oversaw quality control, including checks that cited sources were genuine. They chose the search terms and the distribution of episodes across years; neither was fixed in advance. The stopping rule was to reach at least 500 verified episodes per country.

An episode had to identify a responsible individual actor, a clear political action, and a clear target in an electoral context. At least one official source was required. Material from a public body, campaign, or research institution could qualify, as could original reporting by an established newspaper. Podcasts did not qualify. Three high-quality sources were preferred. Seeking several sources was intended to reduce reliance on any one report and allow cross-checks. Only episodes that passed verification against the inclusion requirements were retained, and each was saved with a source packet. The retained packets contain source links, available publication details, and supporting excerpts or summaries.

Three anonymous human reviewers checked samples of episodes and coding separately, without seeing the existing labels. For the episode check, 100 episodes were selected at random, 50 from each country, and each was assigned to one reviewer. Reviewers checked the actor, action, date, target, whether the episode was distinct, and source support. They opened the originally cited source in 96 cases and checked an independent source in four. All 100 passed the episode checks; 99 had direct source support and one had partial support.

## Coding and validation

OpenAI Codex agents assembled the episodes using the search and verification procedure described above. Three independent model coders using OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Luna Max then classified the records with a fixed form, each working from the same evidence for a case. A classification is used in the analysis when the coders meet the agreement rule specified in the codebook; otherwise it remains unresolved. Sacrifice and stakes follow the same definitions in both countries. In Germany, these questions were omitted from the initial coding run and completed in a later run. The author checked the included cases and coding against the cited evidence and classified three actions as protection only, removing their risk-taking labels. These decisions are recorded as human overrides; the original coder answers are retained. Individual coder records are available for both countries.

The replication package contains the source records, evidence identifiers, available individual coder files, agreed classifications, codebooks, and results saved as data files. Agreement among model coders may still reflect shared mistakes because they belong to the same model family. The separate human check below therefore compares their classifications with judgments made independently. Public documentation also varies with position and action type, and it sometimes leaves the actor's position unclear. This limits which actions can be compared and how well those comparisons describe political behavior beyond the documented cases.

For the separate coding check, two human coders independently recoded each of 40 cases, including 32 random and eight deliberately difficult cases. They agreed on all risk-taking and high-stakes labels, 97.5 percent of protection labels, 95.0 percent of withdrawal labels, and 92.5 percent of both accepted-sacrifice and position labels.

The model was more cautious about position: in eight random cases, both humans agreed on a definite position where the model left it unresolved. Holding the action codes fixed and using those positions left all six differences in the predicted direction. The German risk-taking difference changed from 15.4 to 14.5 percentage points ($p=.023$ to $p=.027$), and the U.S. difference changed from 15.8 to 13.9 points ($p=.012$ to $p=.021$); the other four differences changed by no more than 0.5 points. The replication files contain the anonymous responses and full results.

## Germany

The documented German collection contains 500 cases from 20 study periods between 2005--06 and 2026. It covers municipal, district, state, federal, and European politics, including actions that span levels of government. Each case requires an identifiable actor, a dated action, an electoral or office target, pre-action information for assessing the actor's position, evidence linking the action to the target, and a prior course or feasible alternative. The collection contains 276 actors, 432 actor--target groups, and 178 target decisions.

Evidence comes from original campaign material, official rules and results, public broadcasters, news agencies, polls, and established news reports. Each record preserves source links, dates, excerpts or summaries, and source limits. I store later evidence separately so it does not affect the pre-action record of the action, target, position, or classification.

Coders used only information available before the action. For a five-percent entry target, "close" means within three percentage points. For a two-sided contest, "close" means either a margin within ten points or a contemporary source describing the contest as close. I classify coalition, nomination, and office targets using seat calculations and public commitments. "Far below" means that the record shows no ordinary route to the target before the relevant deadline, while "far above" means a secure position. I code a case as "cannot determine" when the evidence does not support any of these positions.

The final data contain 161 close-below, 72 close-above, 38 far-below, 10 far-above, and 14 at-target cases. The evidence left the position unclear in 205 cases. The position comparisons therefore draw on the 295 cases for which coders could determine it.

Risk taking requires both a new possible gain and a new possible loss. Protection requires an existing target-relevant asset, a concrete threat, an action that blocks it, and an opportunity that the actor gives up; it does not require an above-target position. Withdrawal means that the actor stops pursuing the target. To measure accepted sacrifice, the shared questions record whether the action puts policy value, reputation, resources, or average political support at risk or gives it up. Stakes concern the actor's office, career, entry into parliament, coalition or government survival, and future eligibility. I call stakes high when the record supports a high or exceptional personal consequence.

The main estimate is the difference between the two reported rates. For German protection, I report the counts and difference without a formal test. For the other comparisons, I fit a linear probability model. Its one-sided p-value tests the direction predicted by the theory and accounts for repeated actions by the same actor and repeated observations of the same political target. The raw differences remain central. Checks that give each actor equal weight or omit one study period at a time do not change the broad pattern. Full results, including actor-only and target-only checks, are in the replication files.

## United States

The documented U.S. collection contains 503 cases from the same 20 study periods, covering election-related actions at local, state, party, congressional, and presidential levels. Each record identifies the actor, action, date, target, information available before the action for assessing the actor's position, evidence linking the action to the target, prior course or feasible alternative, and limits of the sources. The collection contains 324 actors, 437 actor--target groups, and 271 target decisions.

The final data contain 49 close-below, 83 close-above, 63 far-below, 28 far-above, and 17 at-target cases; coders could not resolve the position in 263 cases. Polls, prior election results, seat or vote calculations, and contemporary descriptions determine the actor's position. Coders do not infer it from the action or final result.

The action definitions are the same in both countries. The U.S. form records whether pre-action evidence shows policy value, reputation, resources, or average political support put at risk or given up. It records costs visible before the action, not losses known only afterward. I call stakes high when the actor faces a high or exceptional personal consequence involving office, career survival, legislative entry, government survival, or future eligibility.

The U.S. analysis uses the same position comparisons and the same method as the German analysis. Full checks are in the replication files.

The same rule applies in both countries. Small differences between action and report dates, counting conventions, and supported scheduling windows are kept as date ranges. A case enters a comparison when the whole range places it in the same group, such as within the last 30 days or earlier. If the range permits either group, the case remains unresolved for that comparison. These measurements were re-extracted and independently reviewed by GPT-6.1 Sol agents using extra high reasoning effort after a check found missed intervals and inconsistent choices of the relevant decision date. ## Codebook

The thirteen-question form below records where the politician stood before acting, what the action could gain or lose, what the politician put at risk or gave up, and their personal political stakes. Its answers describe the choice; they do not assign a numerical probability of success or establish what caused the action. The U.S. wording is followed by the country-specific German rules and office examples. The original forms are retained in the replication package, and the later German completion of omitted sacrifice and stakes questions is described in Appendix B.2. The instructions and questions given to the coders follow.

Use only the assigned packet. Do not use a later election result for a pre-action answer. Cite one to three packet source IDs for each non-missing answer. Give one short factual reason.

For Q2-Q7, use `no` when the packet does not support `yes`. Use `cannot_determine` only for conflicting evidence or an action that the packet does not establish.

### Q1. Position before the action

**Field:** `position_before_choice`

-   `far_below`

-   `close_below`

-   `at_target`

-   `close_above`

-   `far_above`

-   `cannot_determine`

For a two-sided contest, close means within 10 percentage points or a current source that calls the contest close. For a primary, use the operative qualification, delegate, runoff, or winning boundary. Use `far_below` only when the packet shows no realistic ordinary route. Use `far_above` only when the target is secure. Do not infer position from the action or final result.

Use qualitative descriptions consistently:

-   `close`, `toss-up`, or equivalent language can establish close position when the packet also establishes which side of the target the actor is on;

-   `long shot`, no viable ordinary route, or equivalent language can establish `far_below`;

-   `secure`, `prohibitive favorite`, or equivalent language can establish `far_above`; and

-   `front-runner`, `top tier`, `trailing`, money, incumbency, or candidate status alone does not establish a distance category.

### Q2. Target abandoned

**Field:** `target_abandoned`

-   `yes`

-   `no`

-   `cannot_determine`

Use `yes` when the actor gives up the operative target. A new route to the same target is `no`.

### Q3. New crossing route

**Field:** `new_upside_channel`

Did the action create a concrete route to gain voters, allies, eligibility, office access, votes, or another target-relevant advantage?

-   `yes`

-   `no`

-   `cannot_determine`

Publicity, novelty, effort, and strong language are not sufficient.

Code `yes` only when all three elements are present: a named target-relevant gain, a route changed or added by the action, and a short mechanism from the action to that gain. A routine advertisement, speech, debate, trip, or fundraising act is `no` unless its content or audience supplies that mechanism. A policy or character contrast directed at persuadable voters can be a mechanism. Mere visibility or spending cannot.

### Q4. New downside route

**Field:** `new_downside_channel`

Did the action create a concrete risk of losing voters, allies, eligibility, office access, votes, or target-relevant credibility?

-   `yes`

-   `no`

-   `cannot_determine`

Controversy alone is not sufficient.

### Q5. Downside protection

**Field:** `downside_protection`

Did the action protect an existing voter base, alliance, eligibility route, office position, vote count, or other target-relevant asset from a concrete loss?

-   `yes`

-   `no`

-   `cannot_determine`

The asset must exist before the action. Identify the asset and the loss that the action blocks. A successor route after a personal withdrawal is not protection for the withdrawing actor.

Code `yes` only when the packet supports all three elements:

1.  the actor already holds a target-relevant asset;

2.  a concrete threat could remove or weaken that asset; and

3.  the focal action directly blocks that threat.

Trying to obtain an office, opening a route after a loss, or protecting an imagined future advantage is not downside protection. Refusing to surrender an existing nomination, ballot route, alliance, or office can qualify when the loss threat is concrete.

### Q6. Upside forgone

**Field:** `upside_foregone`

Did the action reject or close a concrete chance to gain voters, allies, eligibility, office access, votes, or another target-relevant advantage?

-   `yes`

-   `no`

-   `cannot_determine`

Do not code an imagined opportunity.

### Q7. Broader cost

**Field:** `broader_cost_supported`

Did the packet or the necessary structure of the action identify a meaningful political, policy, public-welfare, coalition, credibility, trust, or institutional cost outside the immediate target benefit?

-   `yes`

-   `no`

-   `cannot_determine`

This is a foreseeable cost route. It is not proof that harm occurred.

### Q8. Downside intensity

**Field:** `downside_intensity`

-   `low`: narrow, reversible, and mainly actor-borne exposure;

-   `moderate`: meaningful but ordinary office, campaign, coalition, policy, or representation exposure;

-   `high`: a large or difficult-to-reverse loss, a major existing asset at risk, a substantial public cost, or a large downside accepted for narrow upside;

-   `systemic`: a credible risk to elections, legal checks, peaceful transfer, democratic rules, or physical safety;

-   `none_supported`: the packet supports no meaningful downside created or retained by the action;

-   `cannot_determine`.

Judge the added downside relative to the action's supported course context. Do not increase intensity because the target is important, the action is controversial, or the later result is bad. Target abandonment is not itself a downside for this question. A withdrawal is `none_supported` unless it creates or retains a separate cost.

An upside forgone under Q6 or a value spent under Q12 is not by itself an added downside. Use `none_supported` unless the action creates or retains a separate loss route. For example, following a procedure while rejecting a partisan advantage does not create a downside unless the packet supports a separate exposure.

### Q9. Expected effect on core support

**Field:** `core_support_outlook`

-   `likely_strengthens`

-   `mixed_or_neutral`

-   `likely_weakens`

-   `no_relevant_core`

-   `cannot_determine`

The core can be prior voters, party members, an ideological group, or a stable supporting organization. Do not infer detailed preferences from a party label alone. A measured reaction is not required.

Use the actor's most stable pre-action supporters, not all party elites or persuadable voters. Use `likely_strengthens` only for a concrete alignment, mobilization, or retained commitment. Use `likely_weakens` only for a concrete conflict with that core, rejection of its support route, or breach of a clear commitment. Otherwise use `mixed_or_neutral`; this is the default for ordinary campaign activity and withdrawal. Use `no_relevant_core` only when the focal action has no meaningful relationship to the actor's own support base. Use `cannot_determine` only for conflicting evidence or when the relevant core cannot be identified.

### Q10. Expected effect on broader voters

**Field:** `broader_voter_outlook`

-   `likely_attracts`

-   `mixed_or_polarizing`

-   `likely_repels`

-   `no_material_effect`

-   `cannot_determine`

Use the most plausible first-order balance. Concrete outreach, moderation, responsiveness, or coalition acceptability can attract. Obstruction, visible opportunism, a commitment breach, scapegoating, extreme language, or an attack on an institution can repel. A campaign goal or generic publicity is not an attraction route.

### Q11. Expected effect on political trust and democracy

**Field:** `trust_democracy_outlook`

-   `likely_reinforces_trust`

-   `no_material_effect`

-   `risks_political_trust`

-   `risks_democratic_legitimacy`

-   `risks_democratic_institutions`

-   `cannot_determine`

Use the most severe plausible action mechanism.

-   Political-trust risk includes opportunism, a broken expectation, misleading justification, self-protection over a salient problem, or a public-office conflict.

-   Legitimacy risk includes scapegoating, dehumanization, unfair exclusion, or normalization of anti-democratic conduct.

-   Institutional risk includes an attack on a valid election, governing rule, legal check, peaceful transfer, or conduct that enables intimidation or violence.

Accountability, candor, fair procedure, problem-solving, and respect for democratic limits can reinforce trust. Ordinary disagreement has no material effect. This answer is a bounded expectation, not a measured later outcome.

### Q12. Value put at risk or given up

**Field:** `sacrifice_domains`

Choose every supported domain:

-   `policy_value`

-   `reputation_value`

-   `resources`

-   `mean_political_support`

-   `none_supported`

-   `cannot_determine`

Use a value domain when the action, packet facts, or necessary action structure shows that the actor spends, gives up, or foreseeably exposes that value while pursuing or protecting the target. A direct actor admission is not required. The inference must be short and conventional.

-   Policy value includes a policy concession, policy delay, or public-welfare exposure. Merely advocating a policy or risking its electoral rejection is not a policy-value sacrifice.

-   Reputation value includes credibility, commitment, competence, or trust. Ordinary criticism or negative advertising is not sufficient; the packet or necessary action structure must identify the actor's own credibility route.

-   Resources include meaningful money, staff, time, organization, or political capital. Do not count routine campaign activity without a material resource commitment.

-   Mean political support means a likely net support loss across the relevant electorate, not only movement between two subgroups. Use it only when Q10 is `likely_repels` and the action accepts that loss route, or when the packet directly supports a likely net electorate-wide loss.

`none_supported` and `cannot_determine` are exclusive. Do not infer a sacrifice from a bad later result. A withdrawal does not automatically create a value sacrifice.

### Q13. Political stakes

**Fields:** `stake_types` and `stake_intensity`

Choose every supported stake type:

-   `office_value`

-   `career_survival`

-   `legislative_entry`

-   `coalition_or_government_survival`

-   `future_eligibility`

-   `none_supported`

-   `cannot_determine`

Use one intensity for the highest supported personal stake. The permitted values are `low`, `moderate`, `high`, `exceptional`, `none_supported`, and `cannot_determine`.

Do not code career survival merely because an actor is in an election. The packet must connect the choice to continuation, retirement, removal, leadership, or a comparable career boundary. `none_supported` and `cannot_determine` are exclusive. If `stake_types` is `none_supported`, use `none_supported` for intensity. If either part cannot be determined, use `cannot_determine` for both parts.

Stake types and intensity belong to the focal actor, not to the contest in the abstract. Use `office_value` only when the actor personally seeks, holds, or could lose the target office. A surrogate, endorser, or presiding official does not receive the candidate's office stake. Use `legislative_entry` only for a first or renewed route into a legislature. Use `career_survival` only for an explicit continuation, withdrawal, removal, retirement, or comparable career boundary.

Use this intensity scale for the highest supported personal stake:

-   `exceptional`: the actor's own presidency or personal eligibility to assume or retain the presidency;

-   `high`: a U.S. Senate seat, governorship, major statewide executive office, chamber or government control, or a documented career-ending boundary;

-   `moderate`: a U.S. House seat, state legislature, local office, ordinary nomination, or continued campaign;

-   `low`: a minor or mainly symbolic political asset; and

-   `none_supported`: no listed personal stake.

### German rules

For a five-percent entry threshold, close means within three percentage points. For a two-sided contest, close means within ten points or a current source that calls the contest close. Far below requires no realistic ordinary route, and far above requires a secure position.

The German wording of Q12 gives negative coverage as an example alongside ordinary criticism; either is insufficient without a route affecting the actor's own credibility. A foreseeable cost outside the four listed value domains does not qualify as accepted sacrifice. The same value domains and the same personal stake types apply in both countries.

For German Q13, the highest supported personal stake uses the following office examples:

-   `exceptional`: the actor's own position as Federal Chancellor or Federal President, or personal eligibility to assume or retain that national office;

-   `high`: a federal cabinet office, state premiership or major state executive office, national or state government control, or a documented career-ending boundary;

-   `moderate`: a Bundestag, European Parliament, or Landtag seat; a local office; an ordinary nomination; or continuation of an ordinary campaign;

-   `low`: a minor or mainly symbolic political asset;

-   `none_supported`: no listed personal stake; or

-   `cannot_determine`: conflicting evidence or an unestablished holder.

### Summary measures

The majority answers determine the measures used in the analysis:

-   Risk taking requires `yes` to both Q3 and Q4.

-   Protection requires `yes` to both Q5 and Q6.

-   Withdrawal requires `yes` to Q2.

-   Accepted sacrifice requires at least one of the four substantive value domains in Q12.

-   High stakes requires `high` or `exceptional` intensity in Q13.

Unresolved answers are omitted from the corresponding rates. The documented author overrides are applied after deriving these measures.
